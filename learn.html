What is a chromosome count?

It is the number of chromosomes in the cells of an animal. Each species has its own number, and it is one of the simplest facts we can know about a genome.

The short version

A chromosome is a long piece of DNA wrapped up with proteins. Cells keep their DNA in chromosomes. A chromosome count tells you how many a species has. Humans have 46, in 23 pairs.

Two numbers, n and 2n

Most animals get one set of chromosomes from each parent. One set is the haploid number, written n. Both sets together are the diploid number, written 2n. For humans, n is 23 and 2n is 46.

This site shows n by default. You can switch any chart to 2n, which is just n times two.

Some animals break the rule. Male honeybees grow from unfertilized eggs and carry a single set.

Why people count them

  • A count is one of the first things we learn about a genome. It takes a microscope and a well-prepared slide.
  • It is a check for genome assemblies. If an assembly has many more pieces than the species has chromosomes, it is probably not finished.
  • A change in number can help split one lineage from another, so comparing counts across species tells us about how groups diverge.

A bigger number does not mean a more complex animal. The count tells you how the DNA is divided into pieces, not how much the animal can do.

How to read the chart

Each bar is one haploid number. Its height is the number of counts that have it. When you open a species, its bar is amber. The dashed line is the median, the count in the middle when you line them all up. The charts use the counts you choose under Counts to include, High and Medium confidence by default. A species with several counts adds each of them. The table always lists every count.

 

Words you will see

Haploid (n)
One set of chromosomes.
Diploid (2n)
Two sets of chromosomes.
Polyploidy
Having more than two sets. It is common in plants and less common in animals.
Multiple counts
Sources report more than one number for the same species. It can be a mistake. It can also be real, because counts sometimes vary between populations or between males and females. The species page lists every count and every source.
Curated count
The number we cleaned up for the charts. If a source reports a half value, we round it up.

How much to trust a count

Each count has a confidence label: High, Medium or Low. It compares the count with the counts of its relatives in this release. It says how typical the count is for them. It does not say the count is true.

High
The count is within the middle half of the counts of its relatives.
Medium
The count is outside the middle half, but inside the usual range.
Low
The count is outside the usual range.
Not rated
There are too few relatives to compare.

Relatives are the other species in the same genus. If the genus has fewer than five others, we use the family, then the order, the class and the phylum. The page says which group was used. Each relative contributes its first count. The middle half runs from the first to the third quartile. The usual range extends that by 1.5 times its width, with a minimum of 3 chromosomes on each side. Some groups vary a lot, so a Low label does not mean the count is wrong.

Choosing which counts the charts use

The charts have a menu called Counts to include. The default is High and Medium confidence. You can also pick High only, All counts, or Low only, to look at the counts that are flagged. A species with several counts adds each one that passes, so a bar counts counts, not species. The median and the percentages under each chart follow the choice. Counts that are not rated are included, except with High only and Low only. The species table does not follow the menu. It always lists every count.
